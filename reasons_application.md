# 1. The IAEA and the INMA program

## 1.1 The IAEA in a nutshell

- **Origin:** the idea came from the "Atoms for Peace" speech by US President Eisenhower at the UN General Assembly (8 December 1953).
- **Creation:** the IAEA Statute entered into force on **29 July 1957**. The headquarters is in **Vienna, Austria**.
- **Motto:** "Atoms for Peace and Development".
- **Status:** it is an autonomous international organization, inside the UN family. It reports to the UN General Assembly and, when needed, to the Security Council.
- **Director General:** Rafael Mariano Grossi (Argentina), in office since December 2019 and reappointed for a second term in 2023.
- **Nobel Peace Prize:** the IAEA and its former DG Mohamed ElBaradei received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2005.

### The three pillars of the IAEA

| Pillar | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety and Security** | Protect people and the environment from radiation | IAEA Safety Standards (SF-1, SSR-2/2, **SSG-28**) |
| **Science and Technology** | Promote peaceful use of nuclear technology | Nuclear power, medicine, agriculture, **education and training (INMA)** |
| **Safeguards and Verification** | Verify that nuclear material is not diverted to weapons | Inspections under the NPT. In Brazil, together with **ABACC** |

### IAEA Safety Standards hierarchy (useful to explain where SSG-28 is)

- **Safety Fundamentals (SF-1):** the 10 fundamental safety principles. The main objective is to protect people and the environment from harmful effects of ionizing radiation.
- **Safety Requirements (SSR / GSR):** "shall" statements. Example: **SSR-2/2 - Safety of NPPs: Commissioning and Operation**.
- **Safety Guides (SSG / GSG):** "should" statements, how to meet the requirements. Example: **SSG-28 - Commissioning for NPPs**.
- My notes about SSG-28 and RG 1.68 are in [ssg-28_rg-1.68_studies.md](ssg-28_rg-1.68_studies.md).

## 1.2 What is INMA

- **INMA = International Nuclear Management Academy.**
- It is an **IAEA-facilitated framework** that supports universities to create and deliver **master's programmes in Nuclear Technology Management (NTM)**.
- The full name of the program is **INMA-NTM (International Nuclear Management Academy Master's Programmes in Nuclear Technology Management)**.
- **Main idea:** the nuclear industry needs managers who understand both **technology** and **management/leadership**. A good engineer is not automatically a good nuclear manager.
- INMA programmes are designed for people who will take leading roles along the **whole lifecycle of a nuclear facility**, from conception to decommissioning.

### Why the IAEA created INMA

- Many countries are starting or expanding nuclear programmes (newcomer countries).
- The workforce of experienced nuclear managers is getting older (knowledge management problem).
- Traditional engineering courses teach physics and engineering, but not:
  - project and contract management;
  - financing of NPPs;
  - safety culture and leadership;
  - stakeholder involvement and energy policy.
- INMA fills this gap by combining **engineering + management + nuclear safety**.

### Brief history

- **Early 2010s:** the IAEA started developing the INMA requirements together with universities and industry.
- **2015:** first INMA Assist Visits and the first peer review (University of Manchester).
- **2016:** peer review at MEPhI (Russia).
- **2017:** peer review at North-West University and University of the Witwatersrand (South Africa).
- **27 September 2023:** the **KINGS NPP Engineering and Project Management Track** was granted INMA-NTM membership by the IAEA (KINGS 2027 Admission Guide).
- As of February 2024, the IAEA listed **10 member universities**, including MEPhI (Russia), The University of Tokyo (Japan), Texas A&M (USA), North-West and Witwatersrand (South Africa), Budapest University of Technology and Economics (Hungary), National Polytechnic University of Armenia, University of West Bohemia (Czech Republic), **KINGS (South Korea)** and University of Idaho (USA).

### How a programme becomes INMA endorsed

1. The university designs a master's programme in Nuclear Technology Management.
2. The IAEA can perform **Assist Visits** to help the university.
3. The IAEA performs a **peer review mission**. The curriculum is benchmarked against **50 INMA curriculum topics**.
4. If successful, the programme receives the **INMA endorsement** and the university becomes an **INMA member**.
5. Members share educational resources and best practices.

# 2. Nuclear engineering fundamentals, PWRs and the APR1400

This is the most important section for the technical part of the interview. A professor from the area will probably check whether I know the basics.

## 2.1 Basic nuclear physics

### Nucleus, isotopes and binding energy

- Nucleus = protons (Z) + neutrons (N). Mass number **A = Z + N**.
- **Isotopes:** same Z, different N. Example: U-235 and U-238.
- The mass of a nucleus is smaller than the sum of the masses of its nucleons. This **mass defect** is converted into **binding energy**:

$$E = \Delta m \, c^2$$

- The **binding energy per nucleon** curve has a maximum near Fe-56. For this reason:
  - heavy nuclei release energy by **fission**;
  - light nuclei release energy by **fusion**.

### Radioactive decay

- Decay law:

$$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t} \qquad T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

- Activity: $A = \lambda N$ (unit: becquerel, Bq = 1 decay/s).
- Main types: alpha (helium nucleus), beta (electron or positron), gamma (photon), neutron emission.

### Fission

- Example reaction:

$$^{235}\text{U} + n \rightarrow \text{fission products} + (2 \text{ to } 3)\,n + \sim 200 \text{ MeV}$$

- Most of the energy (~165 MeV) is kinetic energy of the fission products, converted into heat inside the fuel.
- **Fissile** isotopes fission with thermal (slow) neutrons: U-235, Pu-239, U-233.
- **Fertile** isotopes can become fissile after neutron capture: U-238 → Pu-239; Th-232 → U-233.
- Natural uranium has only **0.711% U-235**. LWRs use **LEU** enriched to about **3-5%** U-235.
- Rough rule: fission of 1 g of U-235 ≈ 1 MW·day of thermal energy.

### Neutron interactions and cross sections

- A neutron can be **scattered** (elastic or inelastic), **absorbed** (capture or fission).
- **Microscopic cross section** σ (barns, 1 b = 10⁻²⁴ cm²): probability of interaction with one nucleus.
- **Macroscopic cross section**: $\Sigma = N \sigma$ (cm⁻¹).
- **Reaction rate** and **thermal power**:

$$R = \Sigma \, \phi \qquad P = E_f \, \Sigma_f \, \phi \, V$$

where φ is the neutron flux (n/cm²·s) and E_f ≈ 200 MeV per fission.

- U-235 fission cross section is much higher for **thermal neutrons** (~585 b) than for fast neutrons (~1 b). This is why thermal reactors need a **moderator**.

## 2.2 Reactor physics - chain reaction and control

### Multiplication factor

$$k = \frac{\text{neutrons in one generation}}{\text{neutrons in the previous generation}}$$

| k | State | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| k < 1 | Subcritical | Neutron population decreases |
| k = 1 | **Critical** | Steady state (constant power) |
| k > 1 | Supercritical | Power increases |

- **Important:** critical does not mean dangerous. A reactor operating at constant power is critical.

### Six factor formula (finite reactor)

$$k_{eff} = \eta \, f \, p \, \varepsilon \, P_{FNL} \, P_{TNL}$$

- η: reproduction factor (neutrons produced per neutron absorbed in fuel);
- f: thermal utilization factor;
- p: resonance escape probability;
- ε: fast fission factor;
- P_FNL and P_TNL: fast and thermal non-leakage probabilities.
- Without the leakage terms, it is the **four factor formula** for k∞.

### Reactivity

$$\rho = \frac{k_{eff} - 1}{k_{eff}}$$

- Units: Δk/k, pcm (10⁻⁵), or dollars ($1 = β).

### Delayed neutrons - why a reactor is controllable

- About **0.65%** of neutrons from U-235 fission are **delayed** (β ≈ 0.0065). They come from decay of fission products (precursors), seconds after fission.
- **Prompt neutron lifetime** in a LWR is ~10⁻⁴ s. If all neutrons were prompt, power would change too fast to control.
- With delayed neutrons, the effective generation time becomes ~0.1 s, and the reactor can be controlled by rods and boron.
- **Prompt critical:** if ρ ≥ β, the reactor is critical on prompt neutrons alone. This must be avoided by design (Chernobyl is the classic example).
- **Reactor period** (for small reactivity, ρ << β):

$$P(t) = P_0 \, e^{t/\tau} \qquad \tau \approx \frac{\beta - \rho}{\lambda \, \rho}$$

with λ ≈ 0.08 s⁻¹ (one delayed group approximation).

- **Point kinetics equations** (one delayed group):

$$\frac{dn}{dt} = \frac{\rho - \beta}{\Lambda} n + \lambda C \qquad \frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{\beta}{\Lambda} n - \lambda C$$

### Reactivity coefficients (feedback)

| Coefficient | Physical effect | PWR value |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel temperature (Doppler)** | Higher fuel temperature broadens U-238 resonances → more capture | Always **negative**, fastest feedback |
| **Moderator temperature (MTC)** | Hotter water → lower density → less moderation | **Negative** at power (designed to be) |
| **Void coefficient** | Steam bubbles reduce moderation | **Negative** in LWRs |
| **Power coefficient** | Combination of the above | **Negative** |

- **Negative feedback = inherent safety:** if power increases, temperature increases and reactivity decreases. The reactor tends to stabilize itself.
- High boron concentration makes MTC less negative. This is one reason to limit soluble boron.

### Reactivity control in a PWR

- **Control rods** (Ag-In-Cd, B₄C): fast control and shutdown (scram/trip).
- **Soluble boron** (boric acid in the coolant, B-10 absorbs neutrons): slow control, compensates fuel burnup over the cycle.
- **Burnable absorbers** (gadolinium, Gd₂O₃ in the fuel): compensate excess reactivity at the beginning of cycle.
- **Shutdown margin:** the reactor must stay subcritical even if the most reactive rod is stuck out.

### Fission product poisons

- **Xenon-135:** huge thermal absorption cross section (~2.6 × 10⁶ b). After a shutdown, Xe-135 builds up (from I-135 decay), peaking around 10 hours later. This can prevent restart ("xenon dead time").
- **Samarium-149:** stable poison, reaches equilibrium.

### Fuel burnup

- Burnup = energy produced per mass of fuel (MWd/kgU or GWd/tU). LWR discharge burnup is typically ~45-60 GWd/tU.

## 2.3 Thermal-hydraulics and heat removal

- Energy balance in the core or in a steam generator:

$$\dot{Q} = \dot{m} \, c_p \, \Delta T$$

- Heat flux limits the power, not the neutron physics. The main limits are:
  - **DNB (Departure from Nucleate Boiling):** if the heat flux is too high, a vapor film covers the cladding, heat transfer drops, and cladding temperature jumps. PWRs keep a **DNBR (DNB ratio) > design limit** (for example >1.3).
  - **Linear heat generation rate** (kW/m) limited to avoid fuel melting.
  - **Peak cladding temperature** in LOCA: 1204 °C (2200 °F) limit (US 10 CFR 50.46).
- **Peaking factors:** power is not uniform in the core. F_Q (total peaking) and F_ΔH (enthalpy rise peaking) are monitored.
- **Thermal efficiency** (Rankine cycle):

$$\eta = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}} \approx 33\text{-}36\% \text{ for LWRs}$$

  - It is lower than fossil plants because the steam is saturated at lower temperature (~285 °C). Carnot limit: $\eta_{Carnot} = 1 - T_C/T_H$.

### Decay heat

- After shutdown, fission products continue to generate heat. This is the root cause of Fukushima.
- Approximation (Way-Wigner):

$$\frac{P(t)}{P_0} \approx 0.066 \, t^{-0.2} \quad (t \text{ in seconds})$$

- Rough values: ~6-7% just after shutdown, ~1% after 1 hour, ~0.5% after 1 day.
- **Conclusion:** heat removal must work for days and weeks after shutdown.

## 2.4 Nuclear safety concepts

### Three fundamental safety functions

1. **Control of reactivity**;
2. **Removal of heat** from the core and spent fuel;
3. **Confinement of radioactive material**.

### Physical barriers (PWR)

1. Fuel matrix (UO₂ ceramic pellet);
2. Fuel cladding (zirconium alloy);
3. Reactor Coolant System pressure boundary;
4. Containment building.

### Defense in depth (5 levels, IAEA SSR-2/1)

| Level | Objective | Means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prevent abnormal operation and failures | Conservative design, quality in construction and operation |
| 2 | Control abnormal operation, detect failures | Control, limiting and protection systems |
| 3 | Control accidents within the design basis | Engineered safety features, accident procedures |
| 4 | Control severe accidents (beyond design basis), mitigate consequences | Severe accident management, containment protection |
| 5 | Mitigate radiological consequences of large releases | Off-site emergency response |

### Other key concepts

- **Single failure criterion:** a safety system must perform its function even with one single failure. This is why safety systems have redundant trains.
- **Redundancy, diversity, independence and physical separation.**
- **Fail-safe:** control rods fall by gravity when power is lost.
- **Design Basis Accident (DBA):** for PWRs, the classic DBA is the **Large Break LOCA** (Loss of Coolant Accident).
- **Probabilistic Safety Assessment (PSA):**
  - Level 1 → **CDF** (Core Damage Frequency);
  - Level 2 → **LRF / LERF** (Large Release Frequency);
  - Level 3 → consequences to the public.
- **Radiation protection principles:** justification, optimization (**ALARA** - As Low As Reasonably Achievable) and dose limits. Practical rules: **time, distance, shielding**.
- **Doses:** Gray (Gy) = absorbed dose; Sievert (Sv) = equivalent/effective dose. Public limit: 1 mSv/year. Occupational limit: 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years (ICRP).

### Lessons from major accidents

| Accident | Year | Main lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Three Mile Island (USA, PWR) | 1979 | Human factors, control room design, operator training (stuck-open PORV) |
| Chernobyl (USSR, RBMK) | 1986 | Positive void coefficient, no containment, poor **safety culture** (the term was born after Chernobyl, INSAG-4) |
| Fukushima Daiichi (Japan, BWR) | 2011 | External hazards (tsunami), station blackout, **decay heat removal**, need for diverse and mobile equipment (FLEX) |

## 2.5 How a PWR works

```
 PRIMARY LOOP (~15.5 MPa, no boiling)            SECONDARY LOOP (~6-7 MPa, boiling)

 Reactor core --> hot leg (~324 °C) --> Steam Generator --> steam --> Turbine --> Generator
      ^                                      |                           |
      |                                      |                       Condenser (sea/river/cooling tower)
 cold leg (~291 °C) <-- Reactor Coolant Pump <-+                          |
                                                 feedwater <-- feedwater pumps
 Pressurizer: keeps the primary pressure (heaters + spray)
```

- **Principle:** water is the coolant and the moderator. The primary water stays liquid because of the high pressure (~15.5 MPa, 155 bar). Heat goes to the secondary loop through the steam generators. The radioactive primary water does not go to the turbine.

### Main systems

| System | Function |
|---|---|
| **Reactor Coolant System (RCS)** | Reactor vessel, steam generators, reactor coolant pumps, pressurizer, piping |
| **Pressurizer** | Controls RCS pressure (electric heaters to increase, spray to decrease); safety and relief valves |
| **Steam Generator (U-tube)** | Heat exchanger between primary and secondary; a barrier between them |
| **CVCS - Chemical and Volume Control System** | Controls RCS inventory, boron concentration and water chemistry |
| **ECCS / Safety Injection** | Injects borated water in a LOCA (high and low pressure injection, accumulators/safety injection tanks) |
| **RHR / Shutdown Cooling** | Removes decay heat at low pressure and temperature |
| **Auxiliary Feedwater** | Feeds the SGs when the main feedwater is lost |
| **Containment Spray** | Reduces containment pressure and removes iodine |
| **Reactor Protection System** | Automatic trip when a parameter reaches a limit |

### PWR vs BWR - the most common comparison

| Item | PWR | BWR |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling in the core | No | Yes |
| Loops | 2 (primary + secondary) | 1 (direct cycle) |
| Pressure | ~15.5 MPa | ~7 MPa |
| Steam generators | Yes | No (steam separators/dryers inside the vessel) |
| Control rods | Top, by gravity | Bottom, hydraulic insertion |
| Reactivity control | Rods + soluble boron | Rods + recirculation flow |
| Turbine | Non-radioactive steam | Slightly radioactive steam (N-16) |
| Share of world fleet | ~70% | ~15% |

## 2.6 Types of nuclear reactors and their main use

| Type | Moderator | Coolant | Fuel | Main use / examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PWR** (Pressurized Water Reactor) | Light water | Light water | LEU 3-5% | Electricity (most common in the world). **APR1400**, Westinghouse AP1000, EPR, VVER (Russia). **Angra 1 and 2**. Naval propulsion |
| **BWR** (Boiling Water Reactor) | Light water | Light water (boiling) | LEU | Electricity. Japan, USA. ABWR, ESBWR |
| **PHWR** (Pressurized Heavy Water Reactor) | Heavy water (D₂O) | Heavy water | Natural uranium | Electricity. CANDU (Canada), India, **Argentina (Atucha, Embalse)**. Online refueling |
| **GCR / AGR** (Gas-Cooled / Advanced Gas-Cooled Reactor) | Graphite | CO₂ | Natural U / LEU | Electricity (UK, mostly being retired) |
| **RBMK / LWGR** | Graphite | Light water (boiling) | LEU | Electricity (Russia). Chernobyl type |
| **FBR / SFR** (Fast Breeder / Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor) | None | Liquid sodium | MOX / Pu | Breeding fuel, burning actinides. BN-600/800 (Russia) |
| **HTGR** (High Temperature Gas-cooled Reactor) | Graphite | Helium | TRISO particles | Electricity + high temperature process heat, hydrogen. HTR-PM (China) |
| **MSR** (Molten Salt Reactor) | Graphite (some) | Molten salt | Fuel dissolved in salt | Gen IV, under development |
| **SMR** (Small Modular Reactor) | Various (most are LWR) | Various | Various | Electricity < 300 MWe, remote areas, industry, replacing coal. **SMART and i-SMR (Korea)**, NuScale, BWRX-300 |
| **Research reactors** | Various (pool type) | Water | LEU (formerly HEU) | Training, radioisotopes for medicine, material testing. **IEA-R1 (IPEN), RMB (Brazil, under construction)** |
| **Naval propulsion reactors** | Light water | Light water | Highly enriched (US/UK) or LEU (France, **Brazil**) | Submarines and aircraft carriers. Compact PWRs. **LABGENE** is the land-based prototype of the Brazilian submarine reactor |

### Reactor generations

- **Gen I:** early prototypes (1950s-60s). Example: Shippingport.
- **Gen II:** commercial fleet of the 1970s-90s. Example: Angra 1 and 2, OPR1000.
- **Gen III / III+:** evolutionary designs with better safety and economics, 60-year design life, severe accident features. Examples: **APR1400**, AP1000, EPR, VVER-1200.
- **Gen IV:** new concepts (SFR, LFR, GFR, VHTR, MSR, SCWR), focused on sustainability, waste reduction and proliferation resistance.

## 2.7 The APR1400

### What it is

- **APR1400 = Advanced Power Reactor 1400 MWe.**
- **Generation III+** two-loop PWR, developed by **KEPCO and KHNP** (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power), with the Korean industry (KEPCO E&C, Doosan, KEPCO NF).
- **Evolution of:**
  - **OPR1000** (Korean Standard Nuclear Plant, 1000 MWe), the Korean standardized design;
  - **System 80+** (Combustion Engineering, USA), whose technology was transferred to Korea.
- Development took about 10 years (1992-2002). Korean regulatory approval (standard design approval) in 2002.

### Why it matters - the Korean standardization strategy

- Korea did not choose many different designs. It **standardized**: OPR1000 and then APR1400.
- Standardization → repeated construction → learning curve → **lower cost and shorter construction time**.
- This is the main reason for Korea's reputation for **on-time and on-budget** construction.

### Main characteristics (approximate values)

| Parameter | APR1400 |
|---|---|
| Thermal power | 3983 MWt (~4000 MWt) |
| Electric power | ~1400 MWe gross (Barakah: ~1345 MWe net each) |
| Configuration | 2 loops: 2 steam generators, 4 reactor coolant pumps |
| RCS pressure | 15.5 MPa |
| Hot / cold leg temperature | ~324 °C / ~291 °C |
| Fuel | 241 assemblies, 16×16 (PLUS7 fuel) |
| Design life | **60 years** |
| Seismic design | SSE 0.3 g |
| Core Damage Frequency target | < 10⁻⁵ per reactor-year |
| Large Release Frequency target | < 10⁻⁶ per reactor-year |
| Thermal margin | ≥ 10% |
| Target construction time | ~48 months (first concrete to fuel load) |

### Main safety and design features

- **Four-train Safety Injection System with Direct Vessel Injection (DVI):** the water goes directly to the reactor vessel, not to the cold legs, avoiding bypass during a LOCA.
- **IRWST (In-containment Refueling Water Storage Tank):** water source inside the containment. No need to switch from injection to recirculation mode (this was a source of operator error in older plants).
- **Safety Injection Tanks with Fluidic Device:** passive flow control (high flow first, then low flow), reducing the need for low pressure pumps.
- **POSRV (Pilot Operated Safety Relief Valves)** on the pressurizer for safety depressurization.
- **Severe accident features:** cavity flooding system, hydrogen control (passive autocatalytic recombiners and igniters), large dry pre-stressed concrete containment.
- **Fully digital I&C and advanced control room (MMIS - Man-Machine Interface System).**
- **Larger pressurizer** → more tolerance to transients.

### APR1400 units and licensing

| Plant | Country | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Shin Kori 3 & 4** (now Saeul 1 & 2) | Korea | First APR1400s. Unit 3 connected to the grid in 2016. **Reference plant for Barakah** |
| **Shin Hanul 1 & 2** | Korea | In operation (2022, 2024) |
| **Saeul 3 & 4** (former Shin Kori 5 & 6) | Korea | Commissioning / start-up |
| **Barakah 1-4** | UAE | All four in commercial operation (2021-2024) |

- **US NRC Design Certification:** final safety evaluation in September 2018; design certified in **2019** (the first non-US design certified by the NRC).
- **EU-APR:** a modified version that obtained **EUR (European Utility Requirements)** certification in 2017.
- Next Korean designs: **APR1000** (smaller, for Europe; basis of the Czech Dukovany project), **APR+**, and SMRs (**SMART**, **i-SMR**).

### APR1400 at KINGS

- KINGS courses use the APR1400 as a case study, for example "Advanced Power Reactor and Small Modular Reactor" (an INMA compulsory course) and "APR1400 Nuclear Reactor Simulation using Nuclear Design Codes".
- The **Nuclear Plant Analyzer (NPA)** and simulators allow students to see transients and plant behaviour in practice. For me, this is a way to understand the systems I will need to test during commissioning.

### Summing up (my view)

- The APR1400 is the "product" of a national strategy: technology transfer + standardization + repeated construction.
- For LABGENE, the lesson is: a **first-of-a-kind** plant is always harder. Korea reduced risk by repeating the same design. Since we cannot repeat, we need very strong **project management, configuration management and commissioning control**.

## 2.8 Possible interview questions - Nuclear fundamentals, PWR and APR1400

**Q: Explain how a PWR works.**
- A PWR uses light water as coolant and moderator. The primary water is kept at about 155 bar, so it does not boil. The reactor heats the water from about 290 °C to 325 °C. The hot water goes to the steam generators, where it boils the secondary water. The steam drives the turbine and the generator. Then the steam is condensed and pumped back to the steam generators. The pressurizer controls the primary pressure.

**Q: What is the difference between a PWR and a BWR?**
- In a BWR, water boils inside the core and the steam goes directly to the turbine (one loop). In a PWR, there is no boiling in the core and there are two loops, separated by the steam generators. As a consequence, the PWR operates at higher pressure, and its turbine steam is not radioactive.

**Q: What is k_eff? What does "critical" mean?**
- k_eff is the ratio between the number of neutrons in one generation and in the previous one. Critical means k_eff = 1, so the chain reaction is self-sustaining and the power is constant. It is the normal operating condition, not a dangerous one.

**Q: Why is a nuclear reactor controllable?**
- Because of delayed neutrons. About 0.65% of the neutrons from U-235 fission are emitted seconds after the fission. This increases the effective generation time from 10⁻⁴ s to about 0.1 s, and gives time for the control systems to act. Reactivity must always stay below β to avoid prompt criticality.

**Q: What is the Doppler effect? Why are negative coefficients important?**
- When the fuel temperature increases, the U-238 absorption resonances become wider, and more neutrons are captured. So reactivity decreases. Negative coefficients give inherent stability: if power increases, temperature increases and the reactor reduces its own power.

**Q: How is reactivity controlled in a PWR?**
- With control rods for fast changes and shutdown, soluble boron for slow changes like fuel burnup, and burnable absorbers in the fuel.

**Q: What is decay heat? Why is it important?**
- It is the heat produced by the decay of fission products after shutdown. It is about 6-7% of the full power just after shutdown and around 1% after one hour. That is why we need reliable heat removal systems even with the reactor shut down. Fukushima showed what happens when decay heat cannot be removed.

**Q: What are the fundamental safety functions?**
- Control of reactivity, removal of heat, and confinement of radioactive material.

**Q: What is defense in depth?**
- It is a strategy of multiple independent levels of protection and physical barriers, so that one failure does not lead to harm. The IAEA defines five levels, from prevention of failures to off-site emergency response. The physical barriers in a PWR are the fuel pellet, the cladding, the RCS boundary and the containment.

**Q: What is DNB / DNBR?**
- DNB is Departure from Nucleate Boiling. If the heat flux is too high, a vapor film covers the cladding, heat transfer drops and the cladding temperature increases a lot. DNBR is the ratio between the critical heat flux and the actual heat flux. It must be above a limit with margin.

**Q: What is a LOCA? Which systems respond to it?**
- LOCA is a Loss of Coolant Accident, a break in the reactor coolant pressure boundary. The reactor trips and the ECCS injects borated water: the safety injection tanks (passive) and the safety injection pumps. In the APR1400, the water is injected directly into the vessel (DVI) and comes from the IRWST inside the containment.

**Q: What is the APR1400? What are its main features?**
- It is a Korean Gen III+ PWR of about 1400 MWe, developed by KEPCO and KHNP, based on the OPR1000 and System 80+. It has two loops, a 60-year design life, four-train safety injection with direct vessel injection, an IRWST, fluidic devices in the safety injection tanks, severe accident features, and fully digital I&C. It was certified by the US NRC in 2019 and is used in Korea and in Barakah.

**Q: Why are SMRs important? Are they related to your work?**
- SMRs are small reactors (up to ~300 MWe), built with modular components in factories. They can reduce investment and construction risk, and they can be used in remote places or in industry. LABGENE is not an SMR, but it is a compact PWR and a first-of-a-kind facility. Like the first SMRs, it needs strong commissioning management because there is no reference plant.

**Q: What types of reactors do you know? Which is the most common?**
- PWR, BWR, PHWR (CANDU), gas-cooled reactors, RBMK, fast reactors, HTGRs, molten salt reactors, and SMRs. The most common is the PWR, around 70% of the world fleet. Brazil's power plants (Angra 1 and 2) and the LABGENE prototype are all PWRs.

**Q: What is the difference between fissile and fertile material?**
- Fissile material fissions with thermal neutrons, like U-235 and Pu-239. Fertile material becomes fissile after capturing a neutron, like U-238 becoming Pu-239.

**Q: What is ALARA?**
- As Low As Reasonably Achievable. It is the optimization principle of radiation protection: doses must be kept as low as reasonably achievable, considering economic and social factors. Practical tools are time, distance and shielding.

# 3. The Barakah project in the UAE

## 3.1 Overview

- **Barakah Nuclear Energy Plant**, Al Dhafra region, Abu Dhabi, **UAE**. "Barakah" means "blessing" in Arabic.
- **4 × APR1400 units**, about 5.6 GWe total (≈5,350 MWe net).
- **First NPP in the Arab world** and the **first export of Korean NPP technology**.
- Owner: **ENEC** (Emirates Nuclear Energy Corporation). Operator: **Nawah Energy Company**. Regulator: **FANR** (Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation).
- When fully operational, it supplies about **25% of UAE electricity** and avoids about 22 million tonnes of CO₂ per year.

## 3.2 Why the UAE decided to go nuclear

- Strong growth in electricity demand (population and economy). In 2008 the UAE projected demand growing from ~15.5 GWe to more than 40 GWe by 2020.
- Natural gas was needed for exports and petrochemical industry, and part of the gas was imported.
- Imported coal was rejected for environmental reasons, and renewables at that time could cover only a small part.
- Energy security and diversification.

## 3.3 Building the nuclear framework (the "UAE model")

- **April 2008:** the UAE published its **Nuclear Energy Policy**, with strong commitments to transparency, safety, security and non-proliferation.
- **Main commitment:** the UAE **renounced domestic enrichment and reprocessing**. Fuel would be bought from the international market.
- **January 2009:** **123 Agreement** with the USA (peaceful nuclear cooperation). It became known as the "gold standard" of non-proliferation.
- **2009:** creation of **ENEC** (owner/developer) and the **Federal Law on Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy**, which created **FANR**.
- The UAE also joined the **Additional Protocol** with the IAEA and invited many IAEA missions (INIR, IRRS, OSART, Pre-OSART, WANO).
- **Lesson:** the UAE created the legal, regulatory and institutional framework **before** building the plant. This follows the **IAEA Milestones Approach** for newcomer countries.

## 3.4 The tender and negotiations

- **2008-2009:** ENEC invited companies to express interest. Three consortia were shortlisted:

| Consortium | Reactor |
|---|---|
| Areva (France) with EDF / GDF Suez / Total | EPR |
| GE Hitachi (USA/Japan) | ABWR |
| **KEPCO-led "Team Korea"** (KHNP, KEPCO E&C, KEPCO NF, Hyundai E&C, Samsung C&T, Doosan Heavy Industries, Westinghouse) | **APR1400** |

- **27 December 2009:** the KEPCO consortium was selected. The contract for design, construction, commissioning and initial fuel was about **USD 20.4 billion**, largely on a **fixed-price** basis. Korea also expected about USD 20 billion more from 60 years of operation support.
- Korean President **Lee Myung-bak** personally travelled to Abu Dhabi in the final phase. Government-to-government diplomacy was decisive.
- The French offer was considered the favourite at the beginning. Its defeat was a big surprise in the industry.

### Why the UAE chose South Korea

1. **Price:** the Korean offer was much cheaper than the French one (reports at that time indicated a difference of several billion dollars).
2. **Construction time and track record:** Korea had built many reactors on schedule with standardized designs. At the same time, the French EPR projects in Finland (Olkiluoto 3) and France (Flamanville 3) had big delays and cost overruns.
3. **Reference plant:** Shin Kori 3 & 4 (APR1400) were under construction in Korea. The UAE could visit and use them as a reference. The French EPR had no operating reference plant at that time.
4. **Higher capacity factor:** Korean plants had very high capacity factors (above 90%).
5. **Complete package ("turnkey"):** design, construction, fuel, operation support, training, and education of Emirati workers.
6. **Strong government-to-government relationship:** the deal was part of a broader strategic partnership between Korea and the UAE (economy, energy, education and defence cooperation).

## 3.5 Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Dec 2009 | Contract awarded to KEPCO consortium |
| Jul 2012 | Construction start, Unit 1 (first safety concrete) |
| Apr 2013 / Sep 2014 / Jul 2015 | Construction start, Units 2, 3 and 4 |
| 2016 | Creation of **Nawah** (ENEC 82%, KEPCO 18%) as operator |
| 2017 | Operation support agreement: KHNP provides ~400 experienced staff until 2030 |
| 2017-2019 | Unit 1 delayed: operational readiness issues, FANR findings, containment liner voids |
| Feb 2020 | FANR issues the operating licence for Unit 1 |
| Aug 2020 | Unit 1 connected to the grid |
| Apr 2021 | Unit 1 commercial operation |
| Mar 2022 | Unit 2 commercial operation |
| Feb 2023 | Unit 3 commercial operation |
| **Sep 2024** | **Unit 4 commercial operation: all four units in operation** |

## 3.6 Challenges and lessons learned (connection with commissioning)

- **Construction was mostly on time, but the start-up was delayed.** Unit 1 construction was completed around 2018, but the operating licence came only in 2020.
- **Main reasons:**
  - **Operational readiness:** the operator (Nawah) was a new organization. The regulator made more than 400 findings in an operational readiness review.
  - **Workforce and culture:** a multinational workforce. English was the bridge language between Korean and Arab staff, which created communication difficulties.
  - **Technical issues:** voids found in the concrete behind the containment liner in Units 2 and 3 (and later in Unit 1), which needed repair and investigation.
- **Lesson for LABGENE:** finishing construction is not enough. **Operational readiness** (trained people, procedures, management system, safety culture) must be prepared in parallel. This is exactly what SSG-28 says about the transition from construction to operation.
- **Education:** the deal included human resource development. Many Emirati students came to Korea, including to **KINGS**. KINGS was established in 2012, partly as a follow-up of the Korean commitment to train nuclear professionals for newcomer countries like the UAE.

## 3.7 Consequences for Korea

- Barakah became the **showcase** of the Korean nuclear industry abroad.
- It helped Korea win new projects, mainly the **Dukovany II project in the Czech Republic** (KHNP selected in 2024, contract signed in 2025).
- It also showed difficulties: disputes between Korean companies about cost overruns (KHNP and KEPCO went to international arbitration in 2025 over additional costs).

## 3.8 Summing up (my view)

- The UAE succeeded because it followed a clear sequence: **policy → legal framework → regulator → owner → contract → construction → operational readiness → commissioning → operation**.
- Korea won because of **price, schedule, reference plant, standardization and a complete package**.
- The main delay was not construction, but **readiness for operation**. This confirms my interest in commissioning management and the transition between construction and operation.

## 3.9 Possible interview questions - Barakah

**Q: What do you know about the Barakah project?**
- Barakah is the first nuclear power plant in the Arab world, with four APR1400 units built by a KEPCO-led consortium. The contract was signed in December 2009, for about 20 billion dollars. All four units are now in commercial operation, the last one since September 2024. The plant supplies about a quarter of UAE electricity.

**Q: Why did the UAE choose Korea instead of France or the USA/Japan?**
- Mainly because of price, construction time and track record. Korea had a standardized design and a reference plant under construction, Shin Kori 3 and 4. The French EPR projects in Europe had delays and cost overruns. The Korean offer was also a complete package, including operation support and education, and it was supported by a strong government-to-government relationship.

**Q: What were the main challenges in Barakah?**
- Construction was mostly on time, but the start-up of Unit 1 was delayed for about two years. The main reasons were operational readiness of a new operating organization, communication in a multicultural environment, and technical issues like voids in the containment concrete. It shows that commissioning and operational readiness must be prepared in parallel with construction.

**Q: What can Brazil learn from Barakah?**
- First, the importance of a strong and independent regulator, created before the project. Second, the value of standardization and reference plants. Third, operational readiness: people, procedures and management systems must be ready before fuel loading. For LABGENE, which is a first-of-a-kind facility, the third point is especially important.

**Q: What is the "123 Agreement" / "gold standard"?**
- It is an agreement for peaceful nuclear cooperation with the USA, required by Section 123 of the US Atomic Energy Act. The UAE agreement is called the "gold standard" because the UAE renounced domestic enrichment and reprocessing.

**Q: What is the role of the IAEA Milestones Approach?**
- It is the IAEA guidance for newcomer countries. It divides the development of a nuclear programme into three phases and 19 infrastructure issues, like legal framework, regulation, human resources, and stakeholder involvement. The UAE is considered a good example of this approach.

**Q: How is Barakah connected to KINGS?**
- The Korean commitment to the UAE included the education of nuclear professionals. KINGS was created in 2012, and many Emirati students studied there. KINGS is part of the Korean strategy to support newcomer and partner countries.

# 4. The nuclear sector in Brazil

## 4.1 Brief history

| Period | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1951 | Creation of **CNPq** (National Research Council), led by **Admiral Álvaro Alberto**, the "father" of the Brazilian nuclear programme |
| 1956 | Creation of **CNEN** (National Nuclear Energy Commission) |
| 1957 | **IEA-R1** research reactor starts operating in São Paulo (today IPEN). It was the first reactor in the Southern Hemisphere |
| 1962 | Law 4,118: national nuclear energy policy and CNEN as a federal agency |
| 1971 | Construction of **Angra 1** starts (Westinghouse, turnkey contract) |
| 1975 | **Brazil-West Germany Nuclear Agreement** (8 reactors planned, with technology transfer, KWU/Siemens). Creation of Nuclebrás |
| 1979 | Start of the **Brazilian Navy Nuclear Program (PNM)**, which developed the fuel cycle autonomously, in parallel with the official program |
| 1982 / 1985 | Angra 1 first criticality / commercial operation |
| 1986 | Construction of Angra 3 interrupted |
| 1987 | Brazil announces it masters **uranium enrichment by ultracentrifugation** (Navy, Aramar - Iperó, SP). Same year: **Goiânia accident** (Cs-137), one of the worst radiological accidents in the world |
| 1988 | **Federal Constitution**: nuclear activities only for **peaceful purposes**, with approval of Congress (art. 21, XXIII); state monopoly |
| 1991 | Creation of **ABACC** (Brazil-Argentina) and Quadripartite Agreement with the IAEA (in force in 1994) |
| 1997 / 1998 | Creation of **Eletronuclear**. Brazil joins the **NPT** (1998) |
| 2000 / 2001 | **Angra 2** first criticality / commercial operation |
| 2008 | **PROSUB** (Submarine Development Program) with France: 4 conventional submarines and the first **nuclear-powered submarine (SN-BR "Álvaro Alberto")** |
| 2010 | Restart of Angra 3 construction (stopped again in 2015) |
| 2013 | Creation of **Amazul** (Navy company for nuclear and defence technology) |
| 2021 | **Law 14,222/2021** creates **ANSN** (National Nuclear Safety Authority), separating regulation from promotion |
| 2022 | Law 14,514/2022 allows private partnerships in uranium mining (flexibilization of the monopoly). Angra 3 works resume briefly |
| Nov 2024 | **Angra 1 licence extended for 20 years (until 2044)** - long-term operation |
| 2026 | ANSN approves its first **Regulatory Agenda (2026-2027)**. TCU gives 240 days for the government (CNPE) to decide the future of Angra 3 |

## 4.2 Institutional and regulatory framework

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **CNPE** (National Council for Energy Policy) | Defines energy policy, including nuclear decisions (e.g., Angra 3) |
| **MME** (Ministry of Mines and Energy) | Energy policy, Eletronuclear (via ENBPar) |
| **ANSN** (National Nuclear Safety Authority) | **Regulator**: licensing, safety, security and safeguards control of nuclear and radioactive facilities. Created by Law 14,222/2021 |
| **CNEN** | Historically regulator and promoter. Today focused on research, development and promotion (IPEN, CDTN, IEN, IRD) |
| **Eletronuclear** | Operator of Angra 1 and 2, and owner of Angra 3. Controlled by ENBPar |
| **INB** (Indústrias Nucleares do Brasil) | Fuel cycle industry: uranium mining (Caetité, Santa Quitéria), enrichment and fuel fabrication (Resende) |
| **Navy (DGDNTM / DDNM, CTMSP, Amazul)** | Navy Nuclear Program: fuel cycle and naval propulsion (**LABGENE**, SN-BR) |
| **ABACC + IAEA** | Safeguards (verification of peaceful use) |

- **Why ANSN is important:** the IAEA requires an **effectively independent regulator**, separated from organizations that promote nuclear energy (GSR Part 1). Before ANSN, CNEN did both. The separation was a long-standing recommendation.
- **Main legal bases:**
  - Federal Constitution, art. 21 (XXIII), art. 22 (XXVI) and art. 177 (V);
  - Law 4,118/1962 (nuclear policy, CNEN);
  - Law 6,453/1977 (civil liability for nuclear damage);
  - Law 9,765/1998 (licensing fees);
  - Law 14,222/2021 (ANSN);
  - CNEN/ANSN standards (e.g., CNEN NE-1.04 - licensing of nuclear installations; CNEN NN-3.01 - radiation protection).
- **International treaties:** NPT, Tlatelolco Treaty, Convention on Nuclear Safety, Joint Convention, ABACC/Quadripartite Agreement.

| Facility | Type | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Angra 1** | PWR, Westinghouse, ~640 MWe | Operating since 1985; licence until **2044** |
| **Angra 2** | PWR, Siemens/KWU, ~1,350 MWe | Operating since 2001 |

## 4.4 Brazilian electricity matrix and the space for nuclear

### The matrix today

- Brazil has one of the **cleanest electricity matrices in the world**.
- In 2024 (BEN 2025), renewables represented about **88% of electricity generation** and about **50% of the total energy matrix** (world average for total energy is ~15%).
- Approximate shares of electricity generation:
  - **Hydro:** ~55-60%;
  - **Wind + solar:** ~24% (growing very fast);
  - **Biomass:** ~8%;
  - **Natural gas:** ~6-8%;
  - **Nuclear:** ~2-3% (~14-15 TWh/year).
- Angra 1 and 2 together generate a significant part of the electricity consumed in **Rio de Janeiro state**.

### Why nuclear still has space to grow

1. **Hydro is reaching its limits:** most of the remaining potential is in the Amazon region, with strong environmental and social restrictions. New plants are run-of-river, with small reservoirs, so they store less water.
2. **Climate change and droughts:** water crises (2001 energy rationing, 2014-2015 and 2021) showed the vulnerability of a hydro-dependent system.
3. **Wind and solar are intermittent:** the system needs **firm, dispatchable baseload power**. Today, this role is mostly played by **natural gas thermal plants**, which emit CO₂.
4. **Nuclear is low-carbon and firm:** high capacity factor (~90%), stable generation, and plants can be located near the load centres (Southeast).
5. **Uranium reserves:** Brazil has one of the **largest uranium reserves in the world** (~276,000 t U₃O₈), with only about one third of the territory prospected. Brazil is one of the few countries that **masters the whole fuel cycle**, from mining to enrichment and fuel fabrication.
6. **Energy planning:** the **PNE 2050** (National Energy Plan) considers between **8 and 10 GW** of new nuclear capacity by 2050.
7. **New applications:** SMRs for remote areas, industry, mining and data centres; nuclear medicine (RMB).

### Challenges

- **Angra 3 history:** decades of interruptions, high costs and corruption investigations (Operation Lava Jato). It damaged the public image of the sector.
- **High capital cost and long construction time;** financing depends on the government.
- **Public perception** (Goiânia accident, Fukushima).
- **Waste management:** Brazil still does not have a final repository for high-level waste (spent fuel is stored at Angra).
- **Human resources:** ageing workforce and need for new professionals (this is one reason why programmes like KINGS matter).
- **State monopoly:** limits private investment, although it is being discussed and partially relaxed.

## 4.5 Angra 3 - current situation (October 2026)

- About **66% physically complete**, with ~R$ 12 billion already invested. Works stopped since 2015 (except for a short restart in 2022-2023).
- A BNDES study estimated about **R$ 24 billion** more to complete the plant. Keeping the project stopped costs about **R$ 1 billion per year**.
- Eletronuclear suspended debt payments to BNDES until the end of 2027.
- **October 2026:** the **TCU (Federal Court of Accounts)** gave the government **240 days** (120 days for the MME to update the studies and 120 days for the CNPE to decide) to define whether Angra 3 will be completed or abandoned.
- **My view:** Angra 3 shows how a project that stops and restarts many times loses knowledge, increases costs and faces equipment ageing. It is a strong argument for good **project management and planning**, which are the focus of the KINGS INMA track.

## 4.6 The Brazilian Navy Nuclear Program (PNM) and LABGENE

- Started in **1979**, with two main projects:
  1. **Fuel cycle:** mastering uranium enrichment by ultracentrifugation (achieved in 1987). Brazilian centrifuges are also used by INB in Resende.
  2. **LABGENE (Nuclear Energy Generation Laboratory):** a land-based prototype of the propulsion plant of the Brazilian nuclear-powered submarine. It is a compact PWR, located at the Aramar Nuclear Industrial Center (Iperó-SP).
- **Why a land-based prototype?** To test and qualify the reactor, the propulsion plant and the procedures, and to train the operators before installing the plant in the submarine. The US, UK and France followed a similar approach.
- **LABGENE is licensed by the civil regulator** (CNEN, now ANSN), which reinforces Brazil's commitment to transparency and peaceful use.
- Brazil uses **LEU** in its naval reactor, not HEU. Under the Quadripartite Agreement, there are special arrangements with the IAEA for nuclear material used in naval propulsion.
- **The next phase is commissioning.** This is why I am studying SSG-28 and RG 1.68 and why I want to study at KINGS.

## 4.7 Possible interview questions - Brazil

**Q: Can you describe the Brazilian nuclear sector?**
- Brazil has two operating nuclear power plants, Angra 1 and 2, both PWRs, with about 2 GW. Angra 3 is about 66% complete but stopped. Nuclear represents about 2-3% of our electricity. Brazil masters the full fuel cycle and has one of the largest uranium reserves in the world. The Navy has its own program, with the fuel cycle and the LABGENE prototype for the nuclear-powered submarine.

**Q: Brazil already has a very clean and renewable matrix. Why does it need nuclear power?**
- Because most of our renewable generation is hydro, wind and solar, which depend on the weather. Hydro is reaching its limits and recent droughts showed the risk. Wind and solar are intermittent. The system needs firm, dispatchable and low-carbon power. Today this role is played mainly by natural gas. Nuclear can provide baseload power without CO₂ emissions, close to the load centres, using our own uranium.

**Q: What is the situation of Angra 3?**
- It is about two-thirds complete, but works have been stopped for most of the time since 2015. A BNDES study estimated around R$ 24 billion to finish it. In October 2026, the Federal Court of Accounts gave the government 240 days to decide whether to complete or abandon it. It is a lesson about the cost of interruptions in nuclear projects.

**Q: Who regulates nuclear activities in Brazil?**
- Historically, CNEN regulated and promoted nuclear energy at the same time. In 2021, Law 14,222 created ANSN, the National Nuclear Safety Authority, as an independent regulator, following IAEA recommendations. CNEN now focuses on research and development. In 2026, ANSN approved its first regulatory agenda, with topics like long-term operation, ageing management and licensing.

**Q: What is LABGENE?**
- LABGENE is the Nuclear Energy Generation Laboratory, a land-based prototype of the propulsion plant of the future Brazilian nuclear-powered submarine "Álvaro Alberto". It is a compact PWR built by the Brazilian Navy in Iperó, São Paulo. It is a first-of-a-kind facility, and its next phase is commissioning.

**Q: Is the Brazilian nuclear program peaceful? How is it verified?**
- Yes. The 1988 Constitution allows nuclear activities only for peaceful purposes. Brazil is part of the NPT and the Tlatelolco Treaty. Brazil and Argentina created ABACC in 1991, and the Quadripartite Agreement links ABACC and the IAEA. Naval propulsion is a peaceful, non-explosive use of nuclear energy, and the submarine will not carry nuclear weapons.

**Q: What was the Goiânia accident?**
- In 1987, an abandoned radiotherapy source of cesium-137 was opened by scrap collectors in Goiânia. Four people died and hundreds were contaminated. It is one of the worst radiological accidents in history. The main lessons were the control of radioactive sources and public communication.

**Q: What is the future of nuclear energy in Brazil?**
- The National Energy Plan 2050 considers 8 to 10 GW of new nuclear capacity. There are discussions about the completion of Angra 3, the long-term operation of Angra 1 (extended until 2044), SMRs, the RMB research reactor, and more private participation in uranium mining. For me, a key point is to have qualified professionals to manage these projects.

**Q: How will your KINGS degree help the Brazilian nuclear sector?**
- My direct goal is to contribute to LABGENE's commissioning. But the knowledge of project management, licensing and commissioning is also useful for Angra 3, for the RMB, and for future SMR projects in Brazil. Korea has the practical experience in building and commissioning plants that Brazil needs.

**Q: Is there any cooperation between Brazil and Korea in the nuclear field?**
